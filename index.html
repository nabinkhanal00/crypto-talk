Welcome. One message, six eras. Click through them: the scytale only shuffles the letters, Caesar swaps each letter, Vigenère swaps them differently each time, Enigma does it with a machine, and AES (what your phone uses today) turns it into noise.

Three things that are often confused

Hiding a message is not the same as encrypting it.

Only one of these three actually protects you once someone is looking.

Steganography: hide the message itself

Around 499 BC, Herodotus tells us, Histiaeus shaved a trusted servant's head, tattooed a message on his scalp and waited for the hair to grow back before sending him. Here is a modern version. This harmless note hides a meeting time:

Mum enjoyed every tulip. Aunt Tilly noticed our orange narcissi.

Safe only while nobody suspects. Once someone knows the trick, it is read instantly.

Encoding: change the form, no secret needed

Morse code
Base64 (used in emails and web links)
Binary (how a computer stores it)

Not secret at all. Anyone with the public rulebook can reverse it. “It was Base64-encoded” is a real excuse heard after data leaks.

Encryption: scramble it with a secret key

With key 3
With key 11
With key 20

Everyone may know how it works. Without the key, they can't read it. (These use the simple Caesar cipher; we'll see stronger ones.)

Tab 1: the first letters spell MEET AT NOON. The Histiaeus story is from Herodotus. Steganography is still used today (hiding data in images), but it is not a substitute for encryption.

Tab 2: encoding is a translation anyone can undo. Morse and Base64 are public. A common real-world mistake is treating Base64 as protection.

Tab 3: encryption depends on a key. Same message, different key, completely different result. That idea of a small secret key is the core of everything today.

Ancient Sparta · described by Plutarch

The scytale: wrap, write, unwrap.

Wind a leather strip around a wooden rod, write along the rod, then unwind it. The strip is jumbled; only a rod of the same thickness lines the letters up again.

Written along the rod, one row per side:

The unwound strip (what the messenger carries):

The receiver winds it on their rod

A transposition cipher: letters are moved, not replaced. The only key is the rod thickness, so there are just a handful to try.

Plutarch (writing around 100 AD) gives the earliest clear description of how the scytale worked, in his life of the Spartan general Lysander. Some historians think it may have been used more to prove a message was genuine than to hide it.

Demo: change the rod thickness and watch the strip scramble differently. Then move the receiver's slider: only the matching thickness gives the message back. Ask: how many keys does Eve need to try? Only seven here.

Point at the drawing: the front of the rod reads the message, the other rows wrap round the back. Make the rod thicker and it gets fatter, with fewer, wider turns of the strip. This is a transposition cipher: letters are moved, not replaced, so their counts still look exactly like English, which is a giveaway to a codebreaker.

~50 BC · Rome

Caesar: shift every letter.

A different idea from the scytale: instead of moving letters around, replace each one. Turn the inner disc. The outer ring is the normal alphabet; the inner ring is what each letter becomes.

Shift 13 is special: applying it twice gives back the original.

The Roman historian Suetonius wrote that Caesar shifted letters by three. Drag the slider; the inner disc turns. Type in the box and the last letter lights up on both rings.

Ask the room: how many different keys are there? Only 25 useful ones. Next slide: we break it by trying all of them.

Attack #1 · brute force

25 keys? Just try them all.

Eve intercepted this. She doesn't know the shift, so she tries every one. The computer even spots the answer for her, by checking which result has letters as common as normal English.

Lesson 1: if there are few enough keys to try them all, the cipher is broken. So how many is enough? Next slide.

Click "Try every key". The highlighted row is the one whose letters best match the frequencies of English. This "trying every key" attack is called brute force, and it is why key size matters.

Why substitution leaks

Every language has a fingerprint.

In English, E, T and A are everywhere; Q, J and Z are rare. Type or paste any English and its letter chart (filled bars) matches the typical English chart (outlines).

Point at the tall bars for E and T. Then drag the shift slider: the whole fingerprint just slides sideways. That is why Caesar is easy: slide the English pattern along until it lines up, and the distance you slid is the key.

A shuffled alphabet doesn't slide the pattern, it reorders the bars, but the tallest bar is still probably E. That's frequency analysis, next.

9th century · Baghdad · Al-Kindi

Al-Kindi, a scholar in 9th-century Baghdad, wrote the oldest known explanation of this method in "A Manuscript on Deciphering Cryptographic Messages".

Step 1 usually gets only a few letters right on a short text (E and T often land); that's honest: statistics get you started. Step 2 finds the most common three-letter word and assumes it is THE. Then crowd-source the rest, or use Hint.

Story for the room: in 1586, the secret letters of Mary, Queen of Scots, plotting against Elizabeth I, were intercepted and deciphered by the codebreaker Thomas Phelippes. She was executed in February 1587. A cipher you trust but that can be broken is worse than no cipher.

1553 · Bellaso, later credited to Vigenère

Vigenère: a different shift for every letter.

A keyword sets the shift for each position: L shifts by 11, E by 4, M by 12. The same plain letter now becomes different cipher letters, smearing out the fingerprint.

The Vigenère square: find the key letter's row and the plain letter's column; the cipher letter is where they cross.

Called le chiffre indéchiffrable, “the indecipherable cipher”, for about 300 years. Next slide: how it was finally broken.

Click "Next letter" a few times: the key letter picks a row, the plain letter picks a column. Point out that the two Ts in ATTACK become different letters.

Giovan Battista Bellaso described this in 1553; it was later misattributed to Blaise de Vigenère. It resisted codebreakers until the 1800s.

~1854 Babbage · 1863 Kasiski

The weakness: the keyword repeats.

If the keyword has 6 letters, every 6th letter was shifted the same way, so it is just a simple Caesar cipher. Guess the keyword length, and the problem falls apart into easy pieces.

Step 1 · Guess the keyword length

For each guess, split the intercept into that many columns and measure how “English-like” each column is. The right length makes them all look like English again.

Charles Babbage worked this out around 1854 but never published; Friedrich Kasiski published a method in 1863. Lesson: a short repeating key leaks a pattern. Remember this when we reach the one-time pad.

1930s–1945 · Germany · Enigma I

Inside Enigma: follow one letter through the machine.

Pressing a key sends electricity on a journey through scrambling wheels and back. Press → to follow it stage by stage, or type any letter.

    Walk through the six steps with →. Stage by stage: the key, the plugboard (cables that swap pairs of letters), three rotors (each a wheel with 26 contacts on each side and a fixed tangle of wires inside), the reflector (which sends the current back), the rotors again on a different path, the plugboard again, and finally a lamp lights.

    Step 6 is the important one. Because the current comes back through the same parts, pressing the lamp letter gives the original letter (so the same machine decrypts). And because the reflector can never connect a wire to itself, a letter can never encrypt to itself. That small flaw will help break it.

    Then type a few letters on your keyboard: the right-hand rotor turns every time, so the same key takes a different path each time.

    Why the wiring keeps changing

    The rotors turn like a car's mileage counter.

    Every key press turns the right rotor one step before the current flows. When it passes a notch, it kicks the middle rotor along, which in turn kicks the left one. So the machine is a different cipher for every letter.

    Presses so far: 0

    What just happened

      Because of the quirk, the middle rotor sometimes moves twice in a row. The pattern of rotor positions only repeats after 26 × 25 × 26 = 16,900 key presses.

      Press a few times: only the right rotor moves. Then click "Show the quirk": it sets the rotors to A D U and presses three times. Watch: ADV, then AEW (the right rotor passing its notch moves the middle), then BFX (the middle rotor, now at its own notch, moves itself and the left rotor). That "double step" is a real mechanical quirk of the machine.

      Why it matters: with a fresh arrangement for every letter, there is no single letter chart to count. Frequency analysis on its own doesn't work.

      Why the Germans felt safe

      Counting Enigma's possible settings.

      The plugboard contributes the most by far. Oddly, 11 cables would have given more combinations than 13; using all 13 leaves too few unplugged letters to vary.

      A relay race of codebreakers

      How Enigma was broken: Poland first, then Bletchley Park.

      No single genius broke Enigma. Click through the timeline.

      The key messages: the Polish Cipher Bureau, led mathematically by Marian Rejewski, broke Enigma years before the war and gave everything to Britain and France in July 1939. Bletchley Park then scaled it up with Turing and Welchman's bombe machines and a huge, mostly female workforce.

      Equally important: operator habits. Repeating the message key, sending predictable phrases and choosing lazy settings gave the codebreakers their way in. Human behaviour broke the machine as much as maths did.

      1940 · Bletchley Park · Turing & Welchman

      A guessed word plus one flaw.

      Weather stations sent a report every morning containing WETTERVORHERSAGE (“weather forecast”). Slide that guessed word, the crib, along the intercept. Wherever a letter lines up with itself, that position is impossible, because Enigma never turns a letter into itself.

      Every position at a glance (green = still possible). Click one to jump.

      Each surviving position was then tested on Turing's bombe, a room-sized machine that raced through rotor settings looking for ones consistent with the crib.

      Drag the slider: red cells are clashes, so that position is ruled out without touching a rotor. The strip shows all positions; one of the green ones is the real position.

      The flaw from the wiring slide (a letter never becomes itself) plus a predictable message was enough. Modern ciphers are designed so that knowing part of the message tells you nothing about the rest.

      1883 · Auguste Kerckhoffs

      Only the key should be secret.

      A cipher should stay safe even if the enemy knows exactly how it works, as long as they don't have the key. The Germans assumed Enigma's design was secret. Polish mathematicians had worked out its wiring by the end of 1932.

      Which would you trust with your company's data?

      Vote, then click A or B.

      Claude Shannon put it more bluntly in 1949: “the enemy knows the system.” Secret methods get discovered; keys can simply be changed.

      Optional. Take a show of hands before clicking. The point: secrecy of the method is not real protection; public scrutiny is. This is why every serious cipher today is published.

      A two-minute detour

      To a computer, every letter is a row of switches.

      Computers store each letter as a number, and each number as 8 on/off switches called bits. Flip a switch and watch the letter change.

      Each switch is worth double the one to its right: 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. Add up the switches that are on to get the letter's number.

      XOR: the only operation you need today

      Combine a message bit with a key bit: if the key bit is 1, flip the message bit; if it is 0, leave it alone.

      XOR =
      Result1

      The magic trick: XOR with the same key twice and you get the original back. Flip twice, you're where you started. That is what makes the next cipher work.

      For non-engineers: H is number 72, which is 64 + 8, so the switches for 64 and 8 are on. Click switches to change the letter live; turning on the 32 switch makes it lower case.

      XOR is just "flip where the key says flip". Do it twice and you're back. The one-time pad on the next slide is nothing more than this, done with a random key as long as the message.

      1917 Vernam · 1949 Shannon proves it perfect

      The one-time pad: mathematically unbreakable.

      Remember Vigenère's weakness, the repeating keyword? Remove it: use a key that is truly random, as long as the message, and never used again. Then combine with XOR.

      Why Eve can never win

      Eve has this ciphertext. For any message she guesses, there is a key that turns the ciphertext into exactly that message. Every guess is equally believable.

      Ciphertext
      Key that fits

      Gilbert Vernam built this into teleprinters in 1917 (patented 1919). Claude Shannon proved in the 1940s that, used properly, it is perfectly secure: the ciphertext gives Eve no information at all.

      Right side: type any 14-letter guess; a matching key always exists. So why don't we use it everywhere? The key must be as long as everything you'll ever send, and you must get it to Bob secretly first. If you could do that, you could just send the message the same way. How phones solve that key-sharing problem is the first topic of Part 2.

      1943–1980 · the Venona project

      Reuse the pad once, and it's over.

      Two pictures encrypted with the same random key. Each encrypted version on its own is pure noise. Combine the two and the key cancels out, leaving both pictures on top of each other.

      The same mistake still happens in modern software, under the name “nonce reuse”.

      Click "Hide the secrets" first so the room sees only noise, then "Combine". Both pictures appear without anyone touching the key.

      Venona: during the Second World War some Soviet one-time pad pages were reused. From 1943 to 1980, US codebreakers exploited this to read thousands of messages. Perfect in theory, broken by one shortcut in practice.

      Audience experiment

      Where do good keys come from? Not from people.

      Ask the room to call out 30 imaginary coin flips, as randomly as they can. Enter them below, then compare with a real random generator.

      The room's flips

      A real random generator

      In 30 fair coin flips, a streak of 4 or more identical results in a row turns up about — of the time. People tend to switch too often and avoid streaks, which makes their “random” choices guessable.

      Optional, but fun. Typically the room switches between heads and tails much more often than half the time and rarely produces a streak of four. The machine usually does.

      The lesson for keys and passwords: anything a human picks has patterns (Enigma operators chose rotor starts like AAA or keyboard runs; people pick "password1"). Real keys come from cryptographically secure random generators built into every phone and computer.

      The phrase on every chat app

      Who can read your message on its way?

      Your message passes through several hands before it reaches Bob. Press → to add protection, then click any stop to see who sits there and what they can read.

      Mode 1, no encryption: everyone along the way reads "Meet at 7, bring the contract". Mode 2, encrypted on the way (like a website padlock): the café Wi-Fi and the internet provider see gibberish, but the company's server unlocks it, can read it, and locks it again for Bob. Mode 3, end-to-end: only the two phones ever hold the key, so the server only ever handles gibberish.

      Click the server in mode 2 vs mode 3. In mode 2, anyone who breaks into the company, or forces it to hand data over, can read your chats. In mode 3, there is nothing readable to hand over. Note what the server still sees in mode 3: who the message is for, and when. That's called metadata.
